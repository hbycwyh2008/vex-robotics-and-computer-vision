# Portfolio Requirements

Every student maintains a **GitHub/Markdown portfolio** supported by two kinds of engineering evidence:

1. a **Team Engineering Notebook** for the shared robot/system engineering process; and
2. an **Individual Engineering Learning Log** for personal contribution, understanding, and mastery.

The portfolio is where students curate the strongest evidence of what they built, how it works, what broke, how they tested it, what changed, and what they learned.

> A successful team robot does not automatically prove individual mastery.

---

## Why a Portfolio

- Makes engineering thinking visible.
- Builds a real, shareable record of skills.
- Shows evidence of testing, debugging, and redesign rather than only final results.
- Separates team engineering evidence from individual learning evidence.
- Practices documentation habits used by real engineering teams.

---

## Suggested Portfolio Structure

```text
my-vex-portfolio/
├── README.md                       # About you + project index with links
├── projects/
│   ├── 01-basic-driving-robot/
│   │   ├── README.md               # Explanation, evidence, debugging, reflection
│   │   └── code/                   # Your code or pseudocode
│   ├── 02-sensor-autonomous/
│   ├── 03-robot-challenge/
│   ├── 04-color-detection/
│   ├── 05-vision-decision/
│   └── final-integrated/
├── team-notebook/                  # Team Engineering Notebook entries or links
└── individual-learning-log/        # Individual mastery/contribution evidence
```

If the Team Engineering Notebook is maintained physically or in another approved system, the portfolio may link to selected scans/photos or evidence summaries rather than duplicate every page.

## What Each Project Page Must Include

1. **Overview** — what the project is and the driving question.
2. **Goal and constraints** — what the robot/system must accomplish.
3. **How it works** — explain the build, code, sensors, control logic, or vision system in your own words.
4. **Media** — photos and/or a short video/GIF of the robot or vision output.
5. **Testing evidence** — procedure, repeated trials when appropriate, measurements, and results.
6. **Debugging story** — at least one thing that failed, how it was diagnosed, and what evidence supported the diagnosis.
7. **Iteration** — what changed between versions and why.
8. **AI usage disclosure** — if AI tools were used, explain how the suggestions were verified.
9. **Individual contribution** — what the student personally did and can explain.
10. **Reflection** — what was learned, what limitation remains, and what should be tested next.

## Team Engineering Notebook Requirements

The Team Engineering Notebook records the shared engineering process during work, not after the project is finished.

Core pattern:

> **Goal → Design / Change → Test → Problem → Diagnosis → Decision → Retest → Next Step**

Minimum evidence for a normal work session:

- date, lesson/project, team members and roles;
- today's goal;
- one design/build/code/configuration change;
- one test or observation;
- one result, measurement, or failure;
- one next step.

For major engineering challenges, also include:

- prediction or hypothesis;
- defined test procedure;
- repeated trials or comparison data when reliability/accuracy matters;
- debugging evidence;
- justified engineering decision;
- retest evidence;
- remaining limitation.

## Individual Engineering Learning Log Requirements

The individual log answers a different question:

> **What did I personally learn, contribute, and become able to do?**

Students record:

- what they personally worked on;
- one engineering decision they understand;
- one problem the team encountered and how it was diagnosed;
- one specific personal contribution;
- what they can now explain;
- what they could rebuild or repeat;
- their current Mastery Level and evidence;
- one next learning step;
- AI assistance disclosure when relevant.

See [Individual Engineering Learning Log](student-handouts/individual-engineering-learning-log.md).

The teacher may use the log together with an oral explanation, rebuild task, debugging question, or transfer challenge to verify individual mastery.

## Mastery Evidence

This course uses [Mastery Levels 0–5](mastery-levels.md):

- **Level 0 — Exposure**
- **Level 1 — Follow and make it work**
- **Level 2 — Explain while looking**
- **Level 3 — Rebuild with a checklist**
- **Level 4 — Rebuild independently**
- **Level 5 — Modify, debug, and transfer**

A portfolio should show progression over time rather than simply claim a level.

Examples:

- a completed guided build may support Level 1 evidence;
- an annotated explanation may support Level 2;
- a repeatable rebuild/test process may support Level 3;
- an independently recreated subsystem may support Level 4;
- documented diagnosis, redesign, retest, and application to a new problem may support Level 5.

## Markdown and GitHub Expectations

- Use headings, lists, code blocks, and links consistently.
- Commit regularly with clear messages.
- Link code changes to meaningful engineering milestones when possible.
- Use relative links between pages.
- Never commit secrets or API keys.
- Do not upload unexplained photos as a substitute for technical evidence.

See [GitHub / Markdown Portfolio Guide](student-handouts/github-markdown-portfolio-guide.md).

---

## Evidence Quality Rule

> **Build / Program → Test → Record → Explain**

A working robot, one lucky autonomous run, or AI-generated code is not sufficient by itself.

Strong evidence connects:

> **decision → test → result → next decision**

When students claim that a system became more reliable, accurate, consistent, or effective, they should support that claim with repeated tests or controlled comparisons.

---

## How the Portfolio Is Graded

The existing project rubrics remain the primary measure of project quality. The [Engineering Notebook Rubric](rubrics/engineering-notebook-rubric.md) evaluates engineering documentation, evidence, iteration, and reflection.

Mastery Levels 0–5 are a **separate learning-independence framework**. They may be verified through portfolio evidence, individual logs, oral explanation, rebuilding, debugging, or transfer tasks.

Documentation quality, systematic debugging, repeated testing, individual understanding, iteration, and reflection are explicitly valued across the course.
